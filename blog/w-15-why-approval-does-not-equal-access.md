# A Cancer Device Can Be Approved and Still Be Hard to Access: What TTFields Shows About Global Market Access

A medical device can be authorized for use, listed as commercially available and supported by clinical evidence—yet still be difficult for a patient to obtain. Tumor Treating Fields (TTFields) for glioblastoma show why access is more complicated than a simple approval status.

The first distinction is between a product being commercially available and a health system agreeing to pay for it. Novocure's 2026 Corporate Responsibility Report lists Optune Gio as commercially available in 14 markets. Its FY2025 Form 10-K separately identifies nine countries where the company reported national reimbursement at the end of 2025. Those lists are not interchangeable. A company can sell a device in a market without that country having a national public reimbursement decision.

The United States illustrates payer-specific access. The FDA has authorized Optune Gio for defined adult glioblastoma indications, but the Medicare DME MAC policy cited here covers newly diagnosed supratentorial GBM only when specific clinical, timing and use criteria are met. It includes a seven-week initiation window after chemoradiotherapy, a performance-status threshold and a three-month reassessment. The same local coverage policy does not cover recurrent GBM. A device label and a payer policy therefore answer different questions.

Canada offers another example. Health Canada lists an active Optune Gio device licence, while the 2024 CADTH recommendation says reimbursement should be conditional. Its conditions include a 97% price reduction from the fee submitted for the assessment, trained prescribers and support for patients who do not have a caregiver. A positive recommendation is not proof that every province has adopted funding or that the negotiated patient cost is known.

Some countries have built clearer public delivery models. Germany's G-BA decided that TTFields could be provided within statutory health insurance for a defined newly diagnosed GBM pathway, subject to quality requirements. France lists Optune Gio on its reimbursement list for a specified newly diagnosed indication, with specialist and multidisciplinary prescribing requirements. Japan's national insurance coverage applies to newly diagnosed GBM, even though the device's approval history also includes recurrent disease.

Sweden provides a particularly practical example. A national framework agreement starting in June 2026 makes Optune rental available for region-level procurement through 2030. The framework includes accessories, delivery, training, service, repairs and replacement. It requires a trained treating physician to order treatment for a specific patient, and it provides for monthly use-data reporting to the national quality register. The contract prices are confidential, but the implementation model itself is visible.

The United Kingdom shows a different tension. Novocure lists the UK as a commercial market, while NICE guidance says not to offer TTF for newly diagnosed glioblastoma or recurrent high-grade glioma. That does not tell us every possible private or exceptional access route, but it does show why commercial presence cannot be used as shorthand for national clinical endorsement or routine NHS funding.

The central lesson is that access has several steps: regulatory authorization, clinical recommendation, payer coverage, treatment-center readiness, device delivery, patient training and ongoing support. A patient may encounter a barrier at any of them.

A meaningful global comparison should therefore ask more than "Is Optune approved here?" It should ask: For which indication? Who pays? What are the eligibility conditions? Which hospitals can provide it? What support is included? What does the patient pay? And can the patient continue treatment?
