# W 2 Research Note — How TTFields Disrupt Cancer-Cell Division

**Project:** Comprehensive Analysis of TTFields Therapy in Glioblastoma  
**W:** 2 — Mitosis, Spindle Organization & Cytokinesis  

Tumor Treating Fields (TTFields) are often described simply as an anti-mitotic treatment. The experimental literature suggests a more specific and more interesting picture: TTFields interfere with several coordinated structures involved in cell division, particularly the mitotic spindle and the machinery that completes cytokinesis.

Early studies established that alternating electric fields can inhibit cancer-cell proliferation in a frequency-dependent manner and identified a relevant frequency range for glioma models. Later work examined what actually happens to a cell as it enters and progresses through mitosis.

One of the clearest mechanistic studies, published by Giladi and colleagues in 2015, found that TTFields reduce the proportion of polymerized tubulin and interfere with proper mitotic-spindle assembly. Exposed cells subsequently showed abnormal chromosome segregation, multinucleation, and caspase-dependent apoptosis. The effect on cell survival was also influenced by cell division rate and exposure duration.

A separate 2015 study by Gera and colleagues focused on a later point in cell division: the transition from metaphase to anaphase and the execution of cytokinesis. The study found that TTFields disrupted localization of Septin 2/6/7 to the anaphase spindle midline and cytokinetic cleavage furrow. Treated cells developed abnormal membrane blebbing and aberrant mitotic exit.

These findings are complementary. The spindle study helps explain how TTFields can produce chromosome-segregation errors, whereas the septin study helps explain why a cell that has progressed into anaphase may still fail to complete normal division.

The evidence can be summarized as:

**TTFields → disrupted division structures → abnormal mitosis/cytokinesis → mitotic catastrophe → reduced clonogenic survival and cell death**

There is an important caveat. It would be too simplistic to call tubulin or septins the single “target” of TTFields. The studies demonstrate cellular and structural effects, but the complete biophysical mechanism by which alternating electric fields interact with intracellular components is not fully resolved.

The same caution applies when moving from cell culture to patients. These are mechanistic preclinical studies, and established cell lines do not reproduce the full biology of a human glioblastoma.

Taken together, the current evidence supports a strong mechanistic conclusion without pretending that every mechanistic step has been solved: **the vulnerability of dividing tumor cells is central to TTFields biology, and both mitotic-spindle disruption and cytokinesis-associated abnormalities are experimentally supported components of that response.**
