# W 10 Blog Draft — The Costs Hidden Behind a Monthly TTFields Fee

**Project:** Comprehensive Analysis of TTFields Therapy in Glioblastoma  
**W:** 10 — Cost Components and Access Pathway

A monthly price is only one piece of the real cost of a medical device treatment. To understand the economics of Tumor Treating Fields (TTFields), we need to know what the fee includes, how long patients use the device, what support is provided, what payers require, and which costs fall to patients or caregivers.

A useful example is Canada's 2024 CADTH assessment. The sponsor-submitted fee for Optune was CAD$27,000 per month and was described as including the treatment kit and support features. CADTH used this price in its assessment and estimated an incremental cost of CAD$336,902 and 0.37 additional quality-adjusted life-years compared with temozolomide alone. The resulting ICER was CAD$899,470 per QALY. CADTH estimated a 91% to 97% monthly-fee reduction to reach the thresholds it considered. These are assessment inputs and model results from 2024, not a universal current price or proof of the price subsequently paid under a negotiated agreement.

Access is not determined by price alone. Under the cited US Medicare DME local coverage determination, coverage for newly diagnosed supratentorial GBM depends on specified clinical and treatment conditions, including performance status, a timing window, absence of progression, and expected average use of 18 hours per day. Continued coverage beyond the initial three-month period requires a clinical reassessment and objective evidence of adherence and benefit. This is a specific local coverage policy, not a rule that applies to every US payer.

A complete economic analysis needs to map the full pathway: eligibility, authorization, device and kit provision, training, ongoing daily use, array replacement, technical support, visits and monitoring, reassessment, and eventual discontinuation. It must also ask which of these components are included in the monthly fee and which are charged separately. If arrays and support are bundled into the fee, counting them again would inflate modeled cost.

Treatment duration is especially important. The longer the device is used, the greater the cumulative fee can become. At the same time, stopping treatment, progression, tolerance, adherence and survival assumptions all influence modeled cost-effectiveness. These inputs need to be tested in sensitivity analyses rather than hidden inside one average cost.

The patient perspective may differ from the payer perspective. Coverage does not automatically tell us whether a patient faces copayments, travel costs, unpaid caregiver time, or other out-of-pocket expenses. The sources reviewed for this week do not establish one reliable universal patient cost, so the project should not assign a number without plan- and country-specific evidence.

The practical lesson is that **price, payer coverage, and patient affordability are three different questions**. For Week 10, the work is to identify what is bundled into fees, document payer rules and budget impact, and mark the cost inputs that still need primary-source evidence.
