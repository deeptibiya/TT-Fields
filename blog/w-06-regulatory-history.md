# W 6 Blog Draft — How TTFields Moved from Recurrent to Newly Diagnosed Glioblastoma

**Project:** Comprehensive Analysis of TTFields Therapy in Glioblastoma  
**W:** 6 — Regulatory History

Tumor Treating Fields (TTFields) are usually discussed in terms of their proposed mechanisms or clinical outcomes. The regulatory timeline adds another perspective: it shows how the authorised use of the device developed, and it helps separate a new clinical indication from a device update or product-name change.

In the United States, the FDA first approved the NovoTTF-100A system on 8 April 2011 for adults with recurrent supratentorial glioblastoma after chemotherapy. The original indication described the device as a monotherapy option after surgical and radiation options had been exhausted.

The next major change came on 5 October 2015. The FDA expanded the Optune label to include use with temozolomide for adults with newly diagnosed supratentorial GBM following maximal debulking surgery and radiotherapy with concomitant standard-of-care chemotherapy. This was not simply a broader version of the recurrent-disease indication. It addressed a different point in the patient's treatment course and a different treatment combination.

That distinction matters when interpreting trial results. The pivotal recurrent-GBM trial did not establish a statistically significant overall-survival advantage for TTFields monotherapy over physician's-choice chemotherapy. In contrast, EF-14 evaluated TTFields added to maintenance temozolomide after standard chemoradiotherapy in newly diagnosed GBM and reported improvements in progression-free and overall survival. The trial settings and treatment strategies should not be mixed together.

Later FDA supplements illustrate why regulatory timelines need careful reading. A 2014 supplement changed the product name from NovoTTF-100A to Optune. A 2016 supplement approved a smaller/lighter device configuration. A 2018 supplement updated the label with long-term EF-14 clinical outcome data. In 2023, the trade name changed to Optune Gio. More recent supplements reviewed through 2026 include supplier, manufacturing, sterilisation, and component changes. Those are important for device lifecycle oversight, but they are not new proof of clinical benefit or new GBM indications by themselves.

International records need the same care. Japan has a national approval record for NovoTTF-100A from March 2015, and Health Canada's active licence database lists Optune Gio. Novocure reports a 2007 European CE certificate. These are different regulatory routes and should not be described as if each were the same type of approval. In particular, CE marking of a medical device is not equivalent to an EMA marketing authorisation for a medicine.

The main lesson from the timeline is simple: **the indication, the evidence supporting it, and the specific regulatory action all need to be recorded separately**. An indication expansion, label update, trade-name change, and manufacturing supplement have different meanings. Treating them as interchangeable can make a therapy's history look more clinically expansive than the underlying regulatory record supports.

For this project, the next step is to align each authorised use with the clinical study that supported it, then clearly mark remaining questions about real-world effectiveness, adherence, access, and cost.
