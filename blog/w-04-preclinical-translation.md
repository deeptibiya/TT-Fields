# W 4 Research Note — Can Preclinical TTFields Results Be Trusted Across Models?

**Project:** Comprehensive Analysis of TTFields Therapy in Glioblastoma  
**W:** 4 — Preclinical Models, Reproducibility & Translational Relevance  

A preclinical result becomes more convincing when it survives a change in model.

That is particularly important for glioblastoma, where no single experimental system reproduces the biological diversity of a human tumor.

Early TTFields research relied heavily on established cancer and glioma cell lines. These models are useful because investigators can control field frequency, intensity, exposure time, cell-cycle state, and experimental conditions very precisely. They have been central to identifying mitotic and cytokinetic effects.

But cell lines are not miniature glioblastomas.

They do not reproduce the full tumor microenvironment, spatial heterogeneity, vascular interactions, extracellular matrix, or patient-specific tumor architecture.

The evidence becomes more interesting when additional models are examined.

Silginer and colleagues reported TTFields activity in long-term glioma cell lines as well as glioma-initiating cells, with frequency- and intensity-dependent effects and cell-cycle abnormalities preceding cell death.

Even more informative are patient-derived three-dimensional models. Nickl and colleagues developed microtumors on organotypic brain slices, organoids, and tumor-slice cultures. TTFields reduced microtumor growth and Ki67 positivity, while responses remained variable between patient samples. The microtumor models were also more sensitive than monolayer cultures.

That finding matters for two reasons.

First, **model architecture can change apparent treatment sensitivity**.

Second, **patient-to-patient variability persists even in a more biologically relevant system**.

Recent work has gone further, examining primary glioblastoma-cell networks, tumoroids, and brain tumor organoids. These systems suggest that TTFields may affect intercellular tumor networks and calcium signaling as well as proliferation.

But there is another side to translational relevance: the field itself.

TTFields are a physical therapy. The field delivered to a tumor is not perfectly uniform. Computational studies using realistic MRI-derived head models show that tissue conductivity, anatomy, tumor position, necrosis, cerebrospinal-fluid geometry, and electrode arrangement can all influence intracranial field distribution.

So a meaningful translational question is not simply:

**"Does a GBM cell respond to 200-kHz TTFields?"**

It is:

**"Does the biologically relevant portion of that tumor receive an effective field, and how does that exposure interact with the biology of that particular tumor?"**

This gives us a much more realistic framework:

**model biology × field delivery × tumor heterogeneity = translational response**

The evidence is also not uniform in maturity.

The broad anti-proliferative effect of TTFields appears across multiple model classes. The importance of mitotic disruption is supported strongly. Patient-derived models support biological variability. Computational models support substantial variation in field distribution.

But the exact hierarchy of molecular mechanisms is still being worked out.

That distinction will matter as the project moves into the clinical literature.

Preclinical evidence can explain **why a therapy might work**.

Clinical evidence must determine **whether it actually helps patients, by how much, for whom, and at what cost or burden**.

**Phase 1 is now complete. Next: Phase 2, W 5 — pivotal clinical evidence and the transition from laboratory mechanism to patient outcomes.**
