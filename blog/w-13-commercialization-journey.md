# From Prescription to Daily Use: The Commercial Journey of Optune Gio

A treatment does not reach a patient simply because it has been prescribed. With a wearable therapy such as Optune Gio, there are several steps between a clinical decision and the patient actually starting treatment.

The journey begins after diagnosis, surgery where feasible, and chemoradiation. For newly diagnosed supratentorial glioblastoma, Optune Gio is used with temozolomide after completion of radiation therapy with concomitant chemotherapy. The treating team has to decide whether the treatment is appropriate and discuss what using it will mean in everyday life.

That everyday part matters. Optune Gio uses four arrays placed on the scalp. Patients need training and regular array changes: the manufacturer instructs users to change the four arrays at least twice a week, with no set worn for more than four days. Scalp care is also important because irritation is a recognized side effect. Separately, the US Medicare DME MAC policy uses average use of 18 hours a day as its coverage benchmark; that is a payer-specific rule, not a universal requirement across all markets. The patient and caregiver are not just learning how to switch on a device; they are adjusting to a routine that continues at home.

In the United States, Novocure describes a structured support process through MyNovocure. The practice completes a certification step and submits a prescription package with clinical records, insurance information and recent MRI scans. The support team then conducts a benefits investigation and works through insurance authorization. After approval, a Financial Coordinator contacts the patient, an array map is shared with the practice, and a Device Support Specialist delivers the device and provides training. The company also describes ongoing technical support and monthly check-ins.

This helps explain why a prescription is not the same as a treatment start. Documentation, authorization, array planning, delivery and training all have to happen. Each step can introduce delays.

Coverage rules add another layer. Under the US Medicare DME MAC policy for newly diagnosed GBM, treatment has to begin within seven weeks of the later of the last concomitant chemotherapy or radiotherapy dose, and the patient must meet additional clinical criteria. Continued coverage beyond the first three months requires an in-person reassessment between days 60 and 91, with documentation of benefit and objective adherence. These are Medicare-specific coverage rules, not requirements that can automatically be applied to every insurer or country.

The distinction becomes even clearer when comparing markets. Optune Gio has a US indication for newly diagnosed and recurrent GBM, but the Medicare policy cited here excludes recurrent GBM from coverage under that policy. In England, NICE guidance says not to offer tumour-treating fields for newly diagnosed GBM or recurrent high-grade glioma. Authorization, clinical guidance and reimbursement are separate decisions.

The larger point is that commercialization does not end when the device is shipped. It depends on whether patients can access it, learn to use it, manage the daily routine, obtain supplies, receive support and remain connected to their clinical team. A meaningful assessment of adoption therefore needs to look beyond prescriptions and revenue. It should also examine time-to-start, authorization outcomes, sustained use, treatment burden and reasons for interruption or discontinuation.

*This summary is based on public manufacturer, CMS and NICE materials. Manufacturer-described support services are not independent evidence of service performance or improved clinical outcomes.*
