# W 14 — Adoption Barriers and Competitive Context

**Project:** Comprehensive Analysis of TTFields Therapy (Optune Gio) for Glioblastoma  
**Phase:** 4 — Commercialization & Launch  
**Week:** 14  
**Review date:** 17 November 2026  
**Status:** Evidence synthesis draft; survey and retrospective estimates are not population-wide market-conversion rates

## Purpose and scope

Examine why eligible patients may not be offered, may decline, may fail to initiate, or may discontinue TTFields; identify factors that make clinician and institutional adoption more or less likely; and distinguish direct TTFields competition from other treatment pathways and emerging device technologies.

The principal clinical-commercial focus remains Optune Gio in newly diagnosed glioblastoma (GBM). Recurrent GBM is discussed separately because the evidence, clinical pathways and payer rules differ. The competitive scan also notes adjacent field-based devices, but it does not treat preclinical or early-pilot technologies as commercially equivalent alternatives.

## Executive synthesis

TTFields adoption is not explained by one barrier. It is shaped by a linked set of clinical, professional, patient, caregiver, financial, institutional and evidence-confidence factors.

1. **Clinical selection:** performance status and ability to complete the practical treatment routine influence whether clinicians offer treatment. A single-institution 2025 study found poor Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) was the most common stated reason for not offering TTFields in its cohort; this is a clinical-selection finding, not proof that all patients with poorer performance status are ineligible under every label or payer.
2. **Clinician recommendation:** awareness and belief in the treatment's value do not automatically translate into a strong recommendation. Survey work has identified support-service, training, prescription-process and insurance-guidance needs, as well as clinician concerns about physical burden and adherence.
3. **Patient information and access equity:** patient-led national survey data indicate that discussion and timing of TTFields can vary by age, treatment center and other patient-reported characteristics. Delayed discussions and the need to self-advocate are recurring concerns.
4. **Daily-use and caregiver burden:** the wearable system has practical consequences for work, hobbies, mobility, body care, relationships, array changes, scalp care and daily routines. Some patients maintain high use despite this burden; others stop because the burden is not sustainable.
5. **Financial and service continuity:** costs may interrupt treatment even after initiation. A 2026 six-center retrospective cohort reported financial constraints among a subset who discontinued TTFields after concurrent chemoradiotherapy. This is a context-specific finding, not a universal discontinuation rate.
6. **Evidence confidence:** the 2025 ASCO-SNO rapid update says alternating electric-field therapy may be added to adjuvant temozolomide in selected newly diagnosed supratentorial GBM (conditional recommendation; moderate evidence). A 2026 *European Journal of Cancer* perspective raises questions about the lack of sham-controlled trials, comparator selection and the contribution of intensive support services. These are important methodological questions, but a perspective article is not itself a replacement for trial-level appraisal or a guideline.
7. **Competition:** the sources reviewed did not identify a second commercially established TTFields platform for GBM comparable to Optune Gio. The competitive environment is therefore more accurately described as a mix of standard treatment pathways, recurrent-disease options, clinical trials, and a small number of emerging adjacent device technologies. The Oncomagnetic Device is a notable investigational example, but it uses oscillating magnetic fields rather than TTFields and remains in an early, non-randomized pilot study.

**Working conclusion:** adoption depends on clinical suitability, physician confidence, informed patient choice, caregiver capacity, coverage and the ability to sustain treatment. Commercial growth cannot be inferred from prescriptions or active-patient counts alone; the access funnel and reasons for non-initiation or discontinuation need transparent denominators and independent measurement.

## 1. Adoption barriers: what the evidence shows

### 1.1 Clinical eligibility and performance status

Clinical selection is a major upstream determinant. In a retrospective single-institution review of 178 GBM records from 2015–2022, 96 patients were offered TTFields. Among the 82 who were not offered Optune, 66% were reported not to have received the offer because of poor KPS. The same study reported that the insurance provider was not associated with whether treatment was offered (P = 0.86) in that institution's cohort.

Interpretation:
- This supports the importance of functional status and clinical selection in that center.
- It does not show that insurance is irrelevant to actual authorization, affordability, initiation or continuation. "Was offered" and "could access and sustain treatment" are different outcomes.
- It does not establish a universal KPS threshold for all clinical settings, labels or payers.
- The cohort was retrospective and center-specific; the data period predates current practice and coverage arrangements.

The 2025 ASCO-SNO rapid recommendation update states that alternating electric-field therapy may be added to adjuvant temozolomide for people with newly diagnosed supratentorial IDH-wildtype GBM who have completed chemoradiation. This is conditional, not a recommendation that every patient should receive it. Patient goals, performance status, disease location, tolerance, capacity to use the device and local access still matter.

### 1.2 Clinician awareness, knowledge and recommendation

A 2022 survey abstract included quantitative surveys of 130 oncologists and 50 patients/caregivers. Oncologists identified perceived physical difficulty (62%), lack of a support system (60%) and adherence concerns (58%) as barriers to recommending TTFields. Among patients/caregivers, the most frequently cited barrier to initiation was concern that treatment might prevent later clinical-trial participation (64%). Although 92% of oncologists said they often or always discussed two-year survival data and 90% said they often or always discussed five-year survival data, only 26% of patients/caregivers recalled discussing those long-term survival data with their physician.

**Funding/affiliation caveat:** the survey was authored by staff from Novocure's Thought Leader Liaison department. It is useful for understanding reported communication and service barriers, but it is sponsor-affiliated survey evidence and should not be treated as an independent estimate of all neuro-oncology practice.

### 1.3 Patient information, timing and unequal access

A national survey published as a 2025 *Neuro-Oncology* supplement abstract included 525 respondents (77% caregivers and 23% patients). It found age-related differences in whether patients reported being offered TTFields: younger patients were more likely to report an offer. Patients treated at NCI-designated centers and male patients were more likely to report being offered TTFields as part of first-line treatment. Higher loneliness scores were associated with more TTFields-related access or use challenges, although the authors caution that the relationship may be indirect. Qualitative responses described delayed discussions, inadequate information and the burden of self-advocating for the option.

The survey is patient/caregiver-reported and was recruited through survey panels and a patient-led organization. Its respondents were not a random population sample: 66% reported private insurance, 55% had a college degree and 49% received care at an NCI-designated cancer center. It should identify possible inequities and questions for further study, not supply a national prevalence estimate for all US patients.

A separate 2024 patient/caregiver survey abstract (n = 297) reported that 40% of respondents had not discussed TTFields with their clinician; among those who attempted to access it, 29% encountered difficulties, often related to financial constraints and insurance denials. These are different sample data from the 2025 525-respondent abstract and must not be merged into one denominator.

**Commercial implication:** discussion is a prerequisite to informed choice. A product may be indicated and available yet remain absent from the patient's decision because of late referral, clinician unfamiliarity, communication gaps or the burden placed on patients to initiate the conversation.

### 1.4 Treatment burden and patient-reported experience

A two-center observational study published in 2019 assessed patient-reported outcomes in 30 high-grade glioma patients receiving TTFields with chemotherapy. At two months after treatment initiation:
- 71% reported that TTFields affected everyday life at least two to three times per week, up to several times per day.
- Frequent device-specific restrictions involved treatment duration (74%), device size (66%), weight (70%), and array change/bonding (66%; reported mean time 43.6 minutes in the abstract).
- Participants reported restrictions involving body care, hobbies, work, mobility and relationships.
- Despite the burden, 70% said they would recommend TTFields to others and 67% said they would use it again.

This finding illustrates why burden should not be reduced to non-adherence. Patients may report meaningful restrictions while choosing to continue treatment. The study was small, included high-grade glioma rather than only a homogeneous newly diagnosed GBM population, and assessed patients after two months; it is not a population-wide estimate of long-term experience.

A 2026 six-center retrospective study examined 100 newly diagnosed GBM patients who received TTFields during concurrent chemoradiotherapy. Sixty-eight continued TTFields into adjuvant temozolomide, while 32 discontinued after chemoradiotherapy. Among the 32 who discontinued, the documented primary reasons were adherence burden (44%, n = 14), financial constraints (28%, n = 9), mild scalp toxicity (16%, n = 5), physician recommendation (9%, n = 3), and other (3%, n = 1). Skin adverse events were reported in 76% of the overall cohort, mostly grade 1–2.

The study found no statistically significant difference in PFS or OS between its continuation and discontinuation groups. It was retrospective, small, non-randomized and restricted to patients who had received TTFields during concurrent treatment and remained progression-free at the end of radiation. It cannot establish equivalence or prove that continued TTFields has no benefit. Its discontinuation reasons are useful for identifying practical barriers, not for estimating rates across all patients or health systems.

### 1.5 Caregiver and social support

In the 2025 single-institution retrospective review, 58% of patients with documented spousal support initiated TTFields compared with 47% of those without spousal support. This is an association, not proof that spouse/partner status caused initiation. It nevertheless supports a practical point: a treatment involving scalp preparation, array changes, device management and long daily use may be easier for some patients when a caregiver can help.

This should not be translated into a requirement for a spouse or family caregiver. Rather, it identifies the need to assess available support and to avoid making access depend on whether a patient has a particular family structure. Professional support, home-care services, training and accessible instructions may partly address the gap, but their effectiveness should be measured.

### 1.6 Financial, reimbursement and geographic barriers

Week 10 and Week 13 already establish that cost and coverage are jurisdiction-specific and that prescription, authorization, start and sustained use are separate steps. Week 14 adds patient-reported evidence that financial constraints can affect continuation as well as initial access.

The 2026 six-center cohort reported financial constraints in 28% of the 32 patients who stopped after concurrent chemoradiotherapy; the paper describes these patients as self-paying patients unable to sustain device rental and array replacement costs in the adjuvant phase. This finding is relevant to affordability, but the local context and payment arrangements may differ considerably from US insurer coverage or other countries' public systems.

The 2025 national survey also found differences by age, treatment setting and gender in whether TTFields was reportedly offered early. These associations should be treated as signals requiring further investigation. They do not demonstrate discriminatory decision-making or identify the causal mechanism.

### 1.7 Evidence confidence and adoption

A 2026 *European Journal of Cancer* current-perspective article, "Tumor treating fields: Will the evidence gap ever be filled?", argues that the absence of sham-controlled trials makes it difficult to separate the direct effect of TTFields from the additional care and support accompanying use. It also questions comparator quality in some pivotal trials, human dosimetry validation, protocol changes and censoring patterns, and calls for stronger independent post-market evidence.

These points should be evaluated carefully:
- The article is a critical perspective/review, not a new randomized trial.
- Its concerns do not erase the randomized EF-14 result or the 2025 ASCO-SNO conditional recommendation for adding alternating electric-field therapy to adjuvant TMZ in selected newly diagnosed supratentorial GBM.
- The concern about support-care confounding is commercially important because device delivery includes patient services, home support and frequent contact. It raises the need for evidence that can distinguish device-specific effect from the effect of the entire care package.
- Real-world adherence associations should not be interpreted as causal evidence that increasing wear-time alone will necessarily improve survival; healthier patients, stronger support and better prognosis can also influence adherence and outcomes.

This evidence debate is itself an adoption barrier: clinicians need transparent, reproducible evidence and clear counseling materials to discuss benefits, uncertainties, burden and alternatives without overstating or dismissing the therapy.

## 2. Competitive context: separate direct competitors from alternatives

### 2.1 What counts as direct competition?

A direct competitor would ideally have a comparable clinical purpose, target population, mechanism/device category, regulatory standing and commercial availability. A treatment that can be used for GBM is not automatically a direct competitor to TTFields. Standard therapy, salvage drugs, surgical approaches and investigational devices may compete for clinical time, patient attention, payer budgets or trial enrollment while serving different roles.

Within the sources reviewed for this Week 14 snapshot, Optune Gio remains the established marketed TTFields system for GBM. A 2026 critical perspective notes that the main TTFields evidence base has been generated by a single company, without substantial replication from competing manufacturers to date. This is not an exhaustive global device-market census, so the finding should be read as source-scoped.

### 2.2 Emerging adjacent device technologies

**Oncomagnetic Device (BioTex / OncoMAGNETX).** ClinicalTrials.gov record NCT06533163 describes a prospective, multicenter, open-label, non-randomized exposure-time-escalation pilot investigating low-intensity oscillatory magnetic therapy in newly diagnosed GBM. The study is designed to evaluate safety and performance, lists an estimated enrollment of 30 and six German recruiting sites in the record last updated 2 June 2026; primary completion is estimated for November 2027. The registered device is described as a portable, wearable, non-invasive magnetic device. Its proposed spinning oscillating magnetic-field mechanism is distinct from the alternating electric fields used by Optune. No trial results were posted in the registry record reviewed. It is an important emerging adjacent technology, not an established clinical substitute or demonstrated competitor to Optune.

A 2026 preclinical report describes reactive oxygen species-dependent inhibition of mitochondrial complex I, oxidative stress, DNA damage and cell-cycle arrest in cultured glioma cells and a syngeneic mouse model. A 2021 case report described seven end-stage recurrent GBM patients treated under compassionate use. These findings are hypothesis-generating and must not be presented as evidence of clinical efficacy. The registered pilot, not the preliminary case observations, is the appropriate source for the current clinical-development status.

**Electro-Capacitive Cancer Therapy (ECCT; C-Tech Labs Edwar Technology).** The company describes a non-contact electric-capacitance device and states on its own website that Phase I and Phase II clinical trials are ongoing. A 2022 review described an ECCT system developed with Shizuoka University and referred to its use under Japan's Regenerative Medicine Act. That secondary-source statement should not be interpreted as a GBM-specific marketing authorization, a US FDA approval or an equivalence finding. The source is company material plus an older review; in this snapshot no independent comparative GBM outcome dataset or regulator-reviewed GBM indication was verified. ECCT should therefore be logged as an adjacent platform to monitor, not treated as a clinically established TTFields competitor.

**EFE-G100.** The Chinese Clinical Trial Registry lists ChiCTR2000034706, a prospective registered, open-label, randomized parallel study comparing EFE-G100 plus temozolomide with temozolomide alone in newly diagnosed GBM. The entry was registered on 15 July 2020 and last refreshed on 22 July 2020; the registry entry lists a planned enrollment of 330. A 2022 review also describes this device as developed by Jiangsu Hailai Xinchuang Medical Technology. The current recruitment status, results, present regulatory status and commercialization status were not verified in this 2026 snapshot. EFE-G100 is therefore a registered development lead, not a confirmed active or marketed competitor.

### 2.3 Standard treatment pathways are alternatives or comparators, not direct device competitors

**Newly diagnosed GBM.** The core pathway for patients with suitable performance status generally includes maximal safe surgery where feasible, radiotherapy with concurrent temozolomide, and adjuvant temozolomide. The 2025 ASCO-SNO rapid update says alternating electric-field therapy may be added to adjuvant TMZ in selected patients with newly diagnosed supratentorial IDH-wildtype GBM after chemoradiation. TTFields is therefore an add-on to the post-chemoradiation pathway, not a replacement for surgery, radiotherapy or temozolomide.

For older patients, poorer performance status or concerns about toxicity/prognosis, the same guideline identifies other reasonable options in appropriate molecular/clinical contexts, including hypofractionated radiation with temozolomide, radiotherapy alone for some MGMT-unmethylated tumors, temozolomide alone for some MGMT-methylated tumors, or best supportive care. These are clinically tailored pathways, not one-for-one device competitors.

**Recurrent GBM.** There is no single universally preferred regimen. The ASCO-SNO guideline recommends clinical-trial enrollment where possible and makes no recommendation for or against a particular therapeutic strategy given the low certainty of evidence. Depending on previous treatments, molecular findings and goals, clinical approaches may include re-resection or re-irradiation in selected patients, lomustine or other nitrosoureas, temozolomide rechallenge in selected settings, bevacizumab for selected symptom/edema or steroid-sparing goals, and biomarker-directed treatment when a validated actionable target is present. The 2025 SNO-EANO consensus notes that regorafenib's earlier phase II signal was not confirmed by GBM AGILE and that it would no longer be listed by NCCN as a recurrent-GBM option. These therapies are not direct TTFields devices; they are alternative or complementary clinical pathways whose place depends on context.

## 3. What could improve adoption without overstating benefit

The evidence supports testing practical interventions rather than assuming that more promotion alone will solve low adoption.

1. **Earlier, balanced discussion:** incorporate TTFields into the post-chemoradiation discussion when clinically appropriate, with clear explanation of indication, evidence, uncertainty, alternatives, expected daily routine, adverse effects and potential costs.
2. **Clinician workflow support:** use a documented referral and decision aid, named administrative ownership, prescriber training, complete documentation checklists and payer-specific coverage checks.
3. **Equity review:** audit whether eligible patients are offered a discussion across age, sex, socioeconomic status, geography, center type and insurance category. Do not use demographic associations as causal explanations without further analysis.
4. **Patient and caregiver readiness:** assess dexterity, caregiver availability, scalp care, work, mobility, body image, language, health literacy, travel and home electricity/device logistics before initiation.
5. **Financial navigation:** discuss both authorization and patient liability; identify what happens when coverage is denied, delayed or ends. Track self-pay and out-of-pocket interruptions.
6. **Clinical versus technical escalation:** provide a clear pathway for medical concerns, skin toxicity and disease progression; manufacturer technical support should not replace clinician advice.
7. **Independent outcomes collection:** measure time from eligibility discussion to prescription, complete submission, authorization, start, 30/60/90-day use, quality of life, caregiver burden, interruptions and reason-specific discontinuation.
8. **Evidence transparency:** encourage independent replication, publication of negative and null findings, explicit conflicts of interest, consistent comparator definitions and better patient-reported outcome reporting.

## 4. Adoption funnel: metrics needed for an independent market view

The following are proposed metrics, not currently verified company performance indicators:

- **Offer rate:** patients meeting prespecified clinical criteria who are offered a documented TTFields discussion divided by the total number meeting those criteria.
- **Decision rate:** patients choosing treatment divided by patients who received a discussion.
- **Prescription rate:** patients prescribed TTFields divided by patients choosing treatment.
- **Submission completeness:** complete payer submissions on first pass divided by all submissions.
- **Authorization rate:** approved requests divided by decisions, reported separately by payer and plan.
- **Start conversion:** patients starting therapy divided by prescriptions and divided separately by approvals.
- **Time to start:** median days from clinical decision and from authorization to first device use.
- **Persistence:** proportions still using therapy at 30, 60, 90 days, 6 months and 12 months, with reasons for censoring and discontinuation.
- **Use intensity:** average device-recorded hours per day, reported with the rule used to calculate it.
- **Patient/caregiver burden:** validated quality-of-life, treatment burden, social participation and caregiver time measures.
- **Equity:** each funnel measure stratified by age, sex, race/ethnicity where responsibly collected, education, insurance, rurality, distance to center and center type.

A funnel must not use active patients as a proxy for new treatment starts. It must not combine patients who are clinically ineligible, not offered treatment, decline treatment, are denied coverage or discontinue for progression into a single "non-adherence" category.

## 5. Week 14 conclusions

- The strongest adoption barriers visible in the available evidence are clinical selection/performance status, clinician support and confidence, incomplete or delayed patient discussion, daily-use burden, social support and financial continuity.
- Patient-reported burden is real but does not mean all patients reject treatment; the small two-center PRO study shows substantial restrictions alongside high compliance and willingness among many respondents to recommend or reuse therapy.
- Caregiver support appears associated with initiation in one retrospective cohort. It should prompt better support services, not caregiver-dependent eligibility assumptions.
- Financial barriers can emerge after treatment has begun, especially where patients are self-paying.
- New national survey data suggest potential differences in discussion and timing by treatment center, age and gender; the results need replication in representative datasets.
- Optune Gio remains the established TTFields reference for GBM in the reviewed sources. Oncomagnetic therapy is a noteworthy emerging adjacent device, but its registered study is still an early pilot with no posted results. ECCT and EFE-G100 require more independent and current evidence before being counted as established competitors.
- Clinical alternatives must be classified by treatment setting. In newly diagnosed GBM, TTFields is added to the adjuvant treatment pathway; recurrent-disease options are varied and individualized.
- Methodological questions about sham controls, supportive-care effects and independent replication should be recorded as evidence-confidence issues, not converted into categorical claims that TTFields either does or does not work.

## 6. Week 15 handover

Week 15 should examine implementation models and country-level access. Prioritize:
1. Center capability and service infrastructure: who identifies eligible patients, who prescribes, who maps arrays, who trains, and who monitors.
2. Payment and affordability pathways: public payer, private insurance, self-pay, financial assistance, local price and supply continuity.
3. International comparison: authorization, guideline position, reimbursement, import/distribution, center availability and support capacity in each selected jurisdiction.
4. Real-world evidence design: independent registries and minimum reporting standards for offer, authorization, initiation, persistence, use and patient-reported outcomes.
5. A focused update of emerging device trials, particularly Oncomagnetic (NCT06533163), while maintaining separation between magnetic-field therapy and TTFields.

## Source and interpretation limitations

- Conference abstracts and patient-led survey summaries are useful signals but do not substitute for full peer-reviewed analyses or probability samples.
- Retrospective cohorts identify associations and documented reasons, not causal effects.
- The 2019 PRO study was small and included high-grade glioma patients rather than only a single newly diagnosed GBM population.
- The 2026 multi-center cohort was non-randomized; its continuation/discontinuation groups should not be interpreted as a randomized comparison.
- The 2026 evidence critique is a current perspective, not new randomized clinical evidence.
- Company-reported ECCT development claims are not independently validated in this analysis.
- The Oncomagnetic Device trial is investigational, open-label and non-randomized, with no posted results in the registry record reviewed. No clinical efficacy conclusion is warranted.
- "Competitor" is used broadly for market-adjacent alternatives. Only a same-purpose, same-setting, sufficiently mature device with comparable authorization and evidence should be counted as a direct commercial competitor.
