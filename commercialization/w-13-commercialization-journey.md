# W 13 — Commercialization Journey: Patient, Clinician and Access Workflow

**Project:** Comprehensive Analysis of TTFields Therapy (Optune Gio) for Glioblastoma  
**Phase:** 4 — Commercialization & Launch  
**Week:** 13  
**Review date:** 17 November 2026  
**Status:** Evidence-mapped workflow draft; not an observational study of actual patient conversion, persistence or service performance

## Purpose and scope

Map how a patient with glioblastoma may move from clinical consideration of Optune Gio to prescription, payer review, device initiation, home use and follow-up. The emphasis is the US newly diagnosed GBM pathway because the available manufacturer workflow and Medicare coverage policy provide a relatively explicit operational sequence. A short UK contrast is included to show why regulatory authorization, clinical guidance and payer access must be considered separately.

This document is specifically about Optune Gio in GBM. It does not assume that the commercial, regulatory or treatment pathways for Optune Lua or Optune Pax are interchangeable with Gio.

## Executive synthesis

Optune Gio commercialization is not a single transaction between a physician and a patient. It is a multi-stage pathway in which several conditions must align:

1. **Clinical and label fit:** the patient must fall within the relevant product indication and the treating team must decide that treatment is appropriate.
2. **Timing and documentation:** the prescription, clinical records and imaging must be assembled in time for payer review and treatment planning.
3. **Coverage and affordability:** a payer must authorize or cover the treatment, and the patient's financial responsibility must be understood.
4. **Operational readiness:** the clinic and patient need array mapping, device delivery, training and a workable home-use plan.
5. **Sustained use and reassessment:** daily wear-time, skin care, supply continuity, clinical monitoring and payer-specific continuation requirements influence whether therapy can be maintained.

The central commercialization insight is that **prescription is not the same as treatment initiation, and treatment initiation is not the same as sustained use**. Each transition has its own failure points. The company describes a structured US support pathway through MyNovocure, but the public materials do not establish real-world conversion rates, authorization success, time-to-start, adherence persistence or the extent to which support changes clinical outcomes.

## 1. Define the treatment pathway before mapping access

The current Optune Gio healthcare-professional information describes the device for adults aged 22 years or older with histologically confirmed GBM. For newly diagnosed supratentorial GBM, Optune Gio with temozolomide is indicated following maximal debulking surgery and completion of radiation therapy with concomitant standard-of-care chemotherapy. The manufacturer also describes a separate recurrent-GBM monotherapy indication after recurrence and when surgical and radiation options have been exhausted. The HCP information states that Optune Gio may be prescribed only by a healthcare provider who has completed the manufacturer's required certification training.

These are product-indication statements. They do not establish that every payer covers every indicated use, or that all countries recommend or reimburse the device.

### Newly diagnosed and recurrent pathways must remain separate

- **Newly diagnosed GBM:** the main journey mapped in this week. In the US product information, Optune Gio is used with maintenance temozolomide after the initial surgery and chemoradiation pathway.
- **Recurrent GBM:** the product information describes a separate monotherapy use. Do not carry the newly diagnosed payer criteria into this setting. The Medicare DME MAC LCD discussed below explicitly states that recurrent GBM claims for E0766 are not reasonable and necessary under that policy.

The distinction matters commercially: an FDA-indicated use can still encounter a payer-specific coverage barrier.

## 3. The US manufacturer-described support model

The current Optune Gio HCP MyNovocure page describes seven operational steps:

1. **Practice certification:** the practice contacts a Territory Manager for in-person or online certification.
2. **Prescription submission:** the practice submits the completed prescription form, insurance card, history and physical, clinical notes, and current MRI scans with and without contrast.
3. **Benefits investigation:** the support team assesses coverage and then pursues insurance authorization.
4. **Welcome call:** after prescription approval, a Financial Coordinator contacts the patient within 72 hours to discuss financial responsibility and coordinate a start appointment.
5. **Treatment planning:** an array layout/map is shared with the practice.
6. **Patient start:** a Device Support Specialist delivers the device to the home or practice and provides live training. The HCP can track progress through MyLink.
7. **Ongoing support:** the site describes 24/7 phone/email assistance, monthly check-ins about usage and supplies, and practical resources.

**Evidence boundary:** these are manufacturer-described service steps and stated support features. They are useful for reconstructing the intended commercial operating model, but they are not independent evidence that every patient receives each service on time, that coverage is consistently obtained, or that the program improves adherence or survival. The same HCP page states that MyNovocure cannot provide medical advice and directs treatment-related questions to the clinician.

## 4. The treatment workload is part of the product experience

The device's real-world use is not limited to wearing a small monitor. The patient-facing instructions and manufacturer materials describe a wearable system that uses four scalp-mounted transducer arrays. The patient must be trained by qualified personnel before use. The manufacturer instructs patients to change the four arrays at least twice per week, with no more than four days between changes, and to pay attention to scalp hygiene and skin condition. Separately, the cited US Medicare DME MAC LCD defines its initial coverage and continued-coverage adherence benchmark as average use of 18 hours per day. That 18-hour figure is a payer-policy criterion in this context; it should not be represented as a universal requirement for every payer or jurisdiction.

Operationally, the treatment routine can involve:

- preparing and maintaining a clean-shaven scalp for array contact;
- placing and replacing four arrays according to the clinician-provided layout;
- changing arrays at least twice per week, with no more than four days between changes under the referenced user instructions;
- carrying and managing the device, batteries and connection components;
- planning breaks for activities such as showering and array replacement while maintaining the prescribed average use;
- monitoring for scalp redness, itching, irritation or skin damage and contacting the clinical team when medical assessment is needed.

Scalp irritation is identified among the common side effects in the patient-facing safety information. Patient readiness therefore includes practical and caregiver capacity, not merely a willingness to accept treatment after hearing about the mechanism.

The treatment burden should be discussed before authorization and start, not after the device arrives. A support program can reduce technical friction, but it cannot remove the daily treatment routine or replace clinician oversight.

## 5. Payer coverage creates a separate timing and persistence gate

### Medicare DME MAC LCD L34823 — newly diagnosed GBM

The current CMS Medicare Coverage Database page identifies four DME MAC jurisdictions and lists states within those jurisdictions. The LCD is a local coverage determination, not a national coverage determination. It states that initial coverage for newly diagnosed GBM requires all listed criteria:

- histologically confirmed, newly diagnosed, supratentorial GBM;
- maximal debulking surgery when feasible, followed by chemotherapy and radiotherapy;
- TTFields initiation within seven weeks of the later of the last concomitant chemotherapy or radiotherapy dose;
- no progression by RANO criteria;
- Karnofsky Performance Status of at least 70;
- expected average use of 18 hours per day.

For continued coverage beyond the first three months, the practitioner must conduct an in-person reassessment no sooner than day 60 and no later than day 91 after treatment initiation. The practitioner must document continued use and benefit, including objective evidence of adherence. The LCD defines adherence as average use of 18 hours per day, excluding days when a medical need to limit or interrupt treatment has been documented.

The LCD explicitly says recurrent GBM claims for E0766 are not reasonable and necessary under this policy. The product's separate recurrent indication must therefore not be treated as equivalent to Medicare fee-for-service coverage.

### Practical implication

The seven-week initiation window creates a coordination challenge across the neuro-oncology team, practice staff, insurer and manufacturer support team. It means that benefits investigation and record collection are not merely back-office tasks; they can influence whether the patient reaches treatment initiation within the policy window. The 60–91-day review is a second administrative-clinical checkpoint where documentation and objective use information matter.

These criteria must be checked against the applicable payer and current policy at the time of care. They should not be applied automatically to commercial insurance, Medicare Advantage, Medicaid, or non-US systems.

## 6. Why product authorization does not equal market access: UK contrast

NICE guideline NG99 currently states that tumour-treating fields should not be offered for newly diagnosed grade 4 glioma (glioblastoma) or recurrent high-grade glioma. A UK parliamentary answer on 16 June 2026 restated the position and described the NICE prioritisation history: the topic had not been prioritised for an update while relevant trials remained ongoing, with reconsideration expected after publication of key trial evidence.

This is a clinical-guidance and health-system adoption barrier, not evidence that the US device label has changed. The UK example reinforces four separate questions:

- Is the device authorized for the indication in the jurisdiction?
- Does national clinical guidance recommend or discourage its use?
- Is it covered or commissioned by the relevant payer or health system?
- Is the service operationally available at a treating center?

A company-reported market launch or a regulatory certificate alone cannot answer all four.

## 7. Commercialization bottlenecks and proposed performance indicators

The following indicators are proposed for future assessment. They are not reported company performance measures unless a source is added later.

| Journey transition | Proposed indicator | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Clinical consideration → prescription | Number of clinically eligible patients discussed; proportion receiving a prescription | Separates awareness and clinician willingness from actual demand |
| Prescription → complete submission | Percentage of submissions complete on first pass; time to complete documentation | Identifies administrative friction |
| Submission → authorization | Approval, denial and appeal rates by payer and plan type; time to decision | Measures payer access rather than assuming it from an indication |
| Authorization → device start | Median time from approval to start; proportion starting within relevant policy window | Captures operational execution and timing loss |
| Start → early use | Training completion; early wear-time; patient/caregiver confidence | Tests whether onboarding translates into feasible use |
| Early use → sustained use | 30-, 60- and 90-day persistence; average daily wear-time; supply interruptions | Distinguishes starts from continued use |
| Ongoing use → clinical follow-up | Completion of clinical review and required payer reassessment | Measures coordination and continuity |
| Continuation → stop | Documented reasons for interruption/discontinuation, including progression, toxicity, burden, preference, coverage and logistics | Identifies modifiable access barriers and non-modifiable clinical reasons |
| Patient experience | Scalp adverse events, caregiver time, treatment burden, patient-reported quality of life and financial burden | Captures costs not visible in prescription or revenue data |

For any future funnel analysis, denominators must be stated. For example, "starts divided by prescriptions" is not interpretable unless the prescription cohort, payer mix, observation period and exclusions are specified.

## 8. Key findings and open evidence gaps

### Findings supported by the sources reviewed

1. The US manufacturer describes a defined practice-to-patient onboarding process, including provider certification, a structured prescription package, benefits investigation, patient welcome call, array mapping, live training and ongoing support.
2. Treatment initiation depends on a practical workflow, not only a clinical decision. Imaging, insurance documents, authorization, treatment planning and staff coordination all sit between prescription and start.
3. Daily use and array changes create an ongoing patient/caregiver workload. Scalp irritation is a recognized adverse effect that requires counseling and access to clinical advice.
4. Medicare DME MAC coverage for newly diagnosed GBM includes a defined start window and a three-month reassessment gate. Recurrent GBM is excluded from coverage under the cited LCD despite the separate product indication.
5. UK clinical guidance remains materially different from the US commercial pathway: NICE says not to offer TTF for newly diagnosed GBM or recurrent high-grade glioma.

### Important gaps

- No independent public dataset was identified in this review that provides the full funnel from clinically eligible patients to referral, prescription, payer approval, start, and long-term persistence.
- Public MyNovocure materials describe services but do not provide independently audited turnaround-time, authorization-success, service-use or adherence-impact data.
- Actual patient out-of-pocket amounts and the distribution of financial responsibility remain payer- and plan-specific; the prior Week 10 caveat remains.
- The patient/caregiver workload needs independent patient-reported evidence rather than reliance on manufacturer testimonials and educational brochures.
- Country-level access requires separate work on authorization, guideline position, payer reimbursement and service availability; the UK example should not be extrapolated to other European markets.
- The recurrent-GBM pathway needs its own payer and clinical-guidance analysis; the newly diagnosed US Medicare pathway should not be reused as a proxy.

## 9. Week 14 handover

Week 14 should examine adoption barriers and competitive context, using this journey map to prioritize the questions:

1. Which barriers are clinical (eligibility, performance status, progression, adverse effects) versus operational (documentation, training, supply, clinic capability), financial (authorization, cost sharing) or patient-level (daily burden, caregiver support, preference)?
2. What evidence exists from independent studies about adherence, patient experience and clinician adoption?
3. How should TTFields be positioned against standard-of-care pathways and other treatment options without calling every alternative a direct competitor?
4. Which factors can be changed through service design, and which are structural constraints of the treatment or payer environment?

Keep GBM-specific claims distinct from product claims for other cancers.

## Interpretation and source limitations

The MyNovocure workflow and patient device pages are manufacturer sources and are treated as descriptions of the intended service model and device instructions, not independent proof of service effectiveness. CMS and NICE documents represent jurisdiction-specific coverage and clinical-guidance decisions, respectively. The patient journey in this document is a structured synthesis of those sources; it is not a claim that all clinics, insurers or countries follow the same sequence.
