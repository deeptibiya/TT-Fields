# W 3 — Cellular Stress, DNA Damage/Repair & Replication Stress

**Project:** Comprehensive Analysis of TTFields Therapy (Optune®) for Glioblastoma  
**Phase:** 1 — Discovery & Preclinical Evidence  
**W:** 3  

## Research question

Do TTFields induce cellular stress, DNA damage, impaired DNA repair, or replication stress, and how should these mechanisms be related to the mitotic/cytokinetic effects established in W 2?

## Summary

The evidence supports **DNA-damage and replication-stress responses as important additional mechanisms of TTFields biology**, but the strength and disease relevance of the evidence differ by mechanism.

In glioma models, Giladi et al. (2017) found that TTFields applied after radiation or bleomycin exposure delayed resolution of DNA damage, with evidence involving γH2AX, Rad51, ATM, and DNA-PKcs signaling. This supports impaired repair of induced DNA lesions, particularly in the context of combination treatment.

Karanam et al. (2017) reported reduced DNA double-strand-break repair capacity and altered BRCA1-associated signaling in non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) models following TTFields exposure. Because these experiments were not performed in GBM, they provide mechanistic support but should not be treated as GBM-specific evidence.

Karanam et al. (2020) extended this work to replication stress, reporting slower replication-fork progression, reduced expression of MCM6/MCM10 and Fanconi-anemia pathway genes, increased R-loop formation, and enhanced sensitivity to DNA-damaging or DNA-repair-targeting treatments. Again, these experiments were performed primarily in cancer cell models outside GBM.

Taken together, the findings suggest:

**TTFields can perturb DNA damage/repair and replication-associated processes, but the extent to which these effects independently drive clinical activity in glioblastoma remains unresolved.**

## 1. DNA damage versus DNA-repair impairment

These are related but distinct concepts.

**DNA damage** refers to lesions introduced into DNA.

**DNA repair impairment** refers to a reduced ability to resolve those lesions.

This matters because γH2AX accumulation alone cannot establish a complete mechanism: γH2AX can increase in response to several forms of DNA damage and replication-associated stress.

The 2017 glioma study therefore used multiple complementary readouts rather than relying on a single marker. It assessed:

- alkaline comet-assay measures of DNA lesions;
- γH2AX foci;
- Rad51 foci;
- phosphorylated ATM;
- phosphorylated DNA-PKcs; and
- a functional homologous-recombination reporter system.

The investigators reported that TTFields impaired repair of radiation- or chemically induced DNA damage in U-118 MG and LN-18 glioma cells.

## 2. What the glioma evidence actually establishes

The 2017 study is particularly valuable for the GBM-oriented project because the experimental models were glioma cells rather than a distant tumor type.

The study showed that applying 200-kHz TTFields after irradiation increased treatment efficacy and delayed the disappearance of DNA-damage-associated signals. The authors proposed inhibition of homologous-recombination repair as one explanation.

Still, this should be interpreted as **preclinical evidence of impaired repair**, not proof that homologous recombination is the single primary pathway responsible for all TTFields effects.

The study was designed around combination with radiation or bleomycin. Therefore:

**Supported:** TTFields can interfere with resolution of experimentally induced DNA damage in glioma models.

**Not established:** DNA-repair inhibition is the dominant mechanism of TTFields monotherapy in human glioblastoma.

The study also disclosed substantial Novocure involvement: multiple authors were Novocure employees and the senior author held Novocure stock. That does not invalidate the experiments, but it reinforces the importance of independent replication.

## 3. BRCA1 and the DNA-damage-response hypothesis

Karanam et al. (2017) examined TTFields-induced vulnerability in NSCLC cell lines.

The study reported:

- altered cell-cycle distributions;
- reduced expression of genes associated with BRCA1 signaling;
- increased DNA-damage-associated foci;
- increased chromatid aberrations; and
- reduced capacity to repair double-strand breaks after irradiation.

The significance of this work is conceptual: TTFields may create a state in which cancer cells become less capable of dealing with DNA damage.

The disease-specific limitation is equally important.

**These experiments were performed in NSCLC, not glioblastoma.**

Therefore, the finding should be carried into the GBM project as a **mechanistic hypothesis supported by cross-cancer evidence**, rather than as a demonstrated GBM-specific pathway.

## 4. Replication stress

Replication stress describes a condition in which normal progression or maintenance of DNA replication is disrupted.

Karanam et al. (2020) provided experimental evidence that TTFields can generate such a state.

Reported findings included:

- reduced replication-fork progression;
- decreased expression of MCM6 and MCM10;
- reduced expression of Fanconi-anemia-pathway genes;
- increased R-loop formation; and
- increased vulnerability to agents that damage DNA or interfere with DNA repair/replication.

Importantly, the authors used direct DNA-fiber measurements rather than relying only on indirect DNA-damage markers.

That makes the replication-stress claim more persuasive than an interpretation based solely on γH2AX accumulation.

## 5. Why R-loops matter

R-loops are three-stranded nucleic-acid structures containing an RNA–DNA hybrid and displaced single-stranded DNA.

Under normal circumstances, R-loops can participate in transcriptional regulation and other cellular processes. Excessive or persistent R-loops can interfere with replication and genome maintenance.

The Karanam study reported increased R-loop formation during TTFields exposure.

Still, increased R-loops should be interpreted as a **marker and possible mediator of replication stress**, not automatically as proof that R-loops are the initiating target of TTFields.

This distinction is important for keeping the mechanistic model causal rather than circular.

## 6. Connecting replication stress to DNA double-strand breaks

A plausible mechanistic sequence is:

**TTFields → impaired replication-fork progression → stalled/perturbed replication → replication-associated DNA lesions → increased DNA-damage signaling → repair demand exceeding cellular capacity → cell death or loss of reproductive capacity.**

The experimental literature supports several components of this sequence, but not every arrow equally well.

The strongest support comes from direct observations of slower replication-fork progression and increased R-loop formation, together with increased DNA-damage-associated signals.

The exact physical event that initiates replication-fork disruption remains unresolved.

## 7. How W 3 connects to W 2

A central question is whether DNA-damage/replication-stress effects are simply consequences of abnormal mitosis.

The 2020 Karanam study is useful because its gene-expression analysis of mitotic regulators suggested that TTFields-induced mitotic aberrations and DNA-damage/replication-stress events may be **intimately linked but initiated independently**.

This means the current model should not force everything through the W 2 spindle pathway.

A better representation is two interconnected branches:

**Branch A — Cell division**

TTFields → spindle/cytokinesis disruption → chromosome missegregation → mitotic catastrophe

**Branch B — Genome maintenance**

TTFields → replication stress / impaired DNA-repair processes → DNA lesions → genomic instability and loss of viability

These branches may converge downstream on cell death, but they should remain analytically distinct until stronger causal evidence connects them.

## 8. Combination-treatment implications

The DNA-damage and replication-stress findings provide a rationale for combining TTFields with treatments that:

- induce DNA damage;
- inhibit DNA repair;
- increase replication stress; or
- exploit deficiencies in homologous recombination or replication-fork maintenance.

The preclinical studies reported enhanced or synergistic effects with radiation, cisplatin, and PARP inhibition in selected experimental systems.

These findings are scientifically useful as **hypothesis-generating combination evidence**.

They should not be translated directly into a claim of clinical benefit. Combination efficacy depends on treatment sequence, dose, tumor biology, normal-tissue effects, and clinical context.

## 10. Major limitations

### 1. Disease-model mismatch

Only part of the W 3 evidence is directly derived from glioma cells. The BRCA1 and replication-stress studies were conducted primarily in NSCLC or broader cancer models.

### 2. Marker specificity

γH2AX is useful but not uniquely diagnostic of one form of DNA damage. Interpretation should therefore rely on multiple assays.

### 3. Combination-treatment design

The strongest GBM-relevant repair findings involve prior induction of DNA damage with radiation or bleomycin. That limits conclusions about TTFields alone.

### 4. Biological complexity

Replication stress, DNA damage, repair defects, and mitotic catastrophe can reinforce one another. A downstream marker cannot automatically identify the initiating event.

### 5. Industry involvement

The 2017 glioma study includes substantial Novocure involvement and disclosed employment and stock relationships. The 2017 NSCLC study was funded by Novocure. These disclosures should remain visible in evidence appraisal.

## Working mechanistic model after W 3

The evidence currently supports a branching model:

**TTFields**

↓

**Division-associated branch**
→ spindle/cytokinesis disruption  
→ chromosome segregation errors  
→ mitotic catastrophe

**Genome-maintenance branch**
→ replication-fork stress  
→ R-loop formation / altered replication machinery  
→ DNA lesions and altered repair  
→ genomic instability / loss of viability

↓

**Convergent cellular outcome**
→ reduced clonogenic survival and cell death

The evidence does **not** justify claiming that one branch is definitively upstream of the other.

## Evidence gaps to carry forward

1. Replication of replication-stress findings in patient-derived GBM models.
2. Direct experiments distinguishing primary DNA-replication effects from consequences of mitotic abnormalities.
3. Identification of predictive biomarkers for DNA-repair or replication-stress sensitivity.
4. Independent validation of BRCA/Fanconi-pathway effects in GBM.
5. Better characterization of treatment sequencing for rational combinations.
6. Determination of whether chronic TTFields exposure produces adaptive genomic responses or resistance.

## W 3 takeaway

The W 3 evidence broadens the TTFields mechanism beyond mitosis.

**DNA-repair impairment is supported directly in glioma models, while replication-stress mechanisms are supported strongly by experimental work in other cancer models.**

The safest scientific interpretation is therefore not that TTFields “damage DNA” through one defined pathway, but that exposure can disturb **genome-maintenance processes**, including DNA-repair kinetics and replication-fork stability.

This branch now needs to be integrated with the W 2 cell-division mechanism without assuming that one completely explains the other.
